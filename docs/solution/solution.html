Why this problem, and assumptions

An editor watches about 100 minutes per show to pick a handful of clips, and a new show arrives every week. The input pack times every shot, action, sound and line, so the hard parts of a promo can be enforced in code: length, a hook in 3 seconds, a cut before the answer. The soft parts can be tested: does it grab, can a stranger follow it. The tool is built around that split.

  • The script is the edit. CAMERA blocks are the shots, so a cut lands on a shot boundary or between lines, never inside a line. Visual moments are judged from the shot descriptions.
  • Hook means the hook's sentence starts within 3 s. Cliffhanger means the clip ends before the line that answers it, and nothing is said after it.
  • Episodes 1–10 are free (typical for these apps). Promos from them rank higher and are never held back as spoilers. The title ("He was a Duke") and the premise are not spoilers.
  • At most 3 segments, each at least 3 s, in story order. There are no text overlays, so a stitched promo must read straight through.

Pipeline: what goes to AI, code and a human

The LLM judges, code measures. The model only points at block ids (E03-B071). Every timestamp, rule, stitch and de-duplication is code, checked against the parsed script, so a model can never invent a time or a quote.
  • Two independent signals. The first is the LLM's tags. The second needs no model:
    • the writer's emotion tags, mapped to an intensity through word lists (97.8% of tagged lines covered)
    • 114 typed sound cues (a few literally say "cliffhanger peak")
    The tagger never sees the emotion scores, so the two can check each other. Lines the signal flags but the tagger missed get one LLM review.
  • Candidates. Every hook × cliffhanger pair is tried, within an episode and across an episode break (the next episode's opening recap is skipped). Clips are snapped to shots and shortened by dropping filler shots, never lines marked essential. Then the hard rules run, and clips sharing more than half their lines are de-duplicated.
  • Judging.
    • An opening test sees only the first 3 s.
    • A cold viewer sees the whole clip, with names hidden unless someone says them.
    • A grader holding the story bible scores the viewer's comprehension, and rejects clips that answer their own question.
    • The top 15 then meet head to head, in both orders; a win that flips with the order counts as a tie. The posting order is 0.6 × pairwise strength + 0.4 × score, with a penalty for repeating an episode, arc or hook type.

Scale and cost

  • 5 episodes, fresh run: 59 LLM calls on Claude Opus 5.5, about $1.20 and 2 minutes with 4 calls in parallel. Every reply is cached, so a rerun costs $0 and reproduces promos.json byte for byte.
  • A whole 50-episode show: about $7 and 10 minutes (estimated from the measured tokens).
    • Tagging is about $3.70. Moving it to Sonnet 5.5 roughly halves that, and is a one-line .env change; any OpenAI-compatible API also works.
    • The cold viewer on the top 35 is about $1.60.
    • Pairwise (78 calls) is about $1.10.
    • The emotion and sound signal costs nothing: it is word lists in code.
  • Editor time: about 10 minutes reviewing 10 ranked clips with exact in/out points, instead of watching about 103 minutes of episodes.

  • Hard rules, in code, on every candidate: length, hook within 3 s, no answer line or dialogue after the cliffhanger, no cut through a line, segment limits, no recap.   • 46 tests, one per business rule, plus a synthetic "new show" run end to end. It has uppercase labels, Windows line endings and renamed files: the interview scenario.

AI tools, and where they got it wrong

I used Claude Code for the design discussion, code, tests and this document. Claude Opus 5.5 runs inside the tool for the story bible, tagging, the viewers, the grader, pairwise and why-lines. Every number is computed in plain Python and traceable to outputs/. Where the AI got it wrong (full log in docs/ai_log.md):

(1) The tagger marked the show's premise as major twists, which hid the best hook. (2) The simulated viewer said it would stop scrolling for everything. (3) Claude Code wrote a name-hider with two faults: it relabelled characters in a different random order on each run, so the cache missed and verdicts changed, and it split "Mysterious Bearded Man" into three people. I caught it when a promo's score fell from 0.91 to 0.68 with nothing upstream changed. (4) The sound word list matched "crack" inside "crackle", so a fireplace counted as an impact sound. (5) The plan assumed episode recaps repeat lines word for word. They reword them, so recap detection now uses word overlap.
